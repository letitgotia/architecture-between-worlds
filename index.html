I
Period III: 2nd c. BCE to 1453

Case study one

The Silk Road:
architecture through exchange

When monks, merchants, patrons, and pilgrims moved ideas across Central Asia, the built environment responded through layered synthesis. The Giant Wild Goose Pagoda in Xi'an offers one focused example of this encounter.

Agency Form Legacy

Giant Wild Goose Pagoda, Xi'an · 652 CE

Built by the monk Xuanzang to house sutras carried back from India on a seventeen-year pilgrimage, the Pagoda translates the solid mass of the Indian stupa into a Chinese timber-frame logic, then realizes it in masonry.

This translation emerged through pilgrimage, monastic networks, local building practice, and Tang patronage. The political condition was relatively open compared with later state-led export programs, and the architecture reflects that layered context.

Political reading

"The Pagoda is hybrid because its form passed through several hands: monks, patrons, translators, builders, and later viewers."

Tang China in 652 was a major imperial power, but Buddhist texts, Sanskrit learning, and Indian iconography also moved through networks the state did not fully control: monastic orders, Sogdian merchants, Kushan-era translators, and pilgrimage routes. The court could patronize Xuanzang's project, but the form was also shaped by transregional religious exchange and local craft.

The cultures meeting here were not equal in every respect, yet none of the relevant traditions can be treated as passive. Tang China, Gupta and post-Gupta India, Sasanian and Central Asian networks, and Sogdian city-states all participated in different ways. The form was negotiated by the masons, monks, patrons, and communities who made it meaningful.

This is why, seventy generations later, the Pagoda is often read within Chinese architectural memory while still carrying traces of Buddhist movement from India. Its legacy is not simply absorption, but a long process of translation, reuse, and reinterpretation.

Case study two

The Cold War:
architecture through state policy

Opened in Warsaw in 1955 as an official Soviet gift to Poland, the Palace of Culture and Science shows how Cold War architecture could operate as both urban infrastructure and political symbol. Its form was not simply copied from Moscow, but neither did it emerge from local architectural culture alone.

Agency Form Legacy

Palace of Culture and Science
Warsaw · 1955

Designed by Soviet architect Lev Rudnev and opened in 1955, the Palace of Culture and Science was officially presented as a Soviet gift to Poland. Its construction involved thousands of Soviet workers, alongside Polish labour, and the building remained Poland's tallest structure for many decades. Its form belongs to the Stalinist high-rise tradition associated with Moscow's post-war skyscrapers, combining stepped vertical massing, socialist-realist monumentality and historicist ornament.

Although the Palace drew on Polish architectural motifs, especially Renaissance and historicist details, its overall scale, urban placement and political origin marked it as a Soviet intervention in the centre of Warsaw. The building therefore occupies an ambiguous position: it is not a direct replica of Moscow's Seven Sisters, but neither is it a locally generated architectural synthesis. It is better understood as a Soviet monumental form adapted with Polish decorative references.

Political reading

The Palace can be read as an architectural expression of asymmetric power within the Soviet sphere of influence. Its official status as a "gift" masked the unequal political conditions under which it was conceived, financed and built. Polish actors contributed to aspects of its function, location and decorative language, but the dominant architectural type and symbolic logic came from Moscow.

Seventy years later, the building remains contested. For some, it continues to signify Soviet domination; for others, especially younger residents, it has become part of Warsaw's urban identity. This unresolved status is precisely what makes the Palace historically significant: it shows how architecture can outlast the political regime that produced it, while continuing to carry traces of that regime in its form, scale and urban presence.

Findings

What the stones
remember

Architectural encounters are shaped by trade, belief, institutions, labour, materials, political authority, and later interpretation. Their meanings are rarely singular: a building can support identity and exchange while also recording unequal access, contested memory, or institutional power.

"Architecture does not carry one message forever. It gathers meanings as people build it, use it, govern it, remember it, and argue over it."

Read together, Period III and Period VI suggest a comparative finding rather than a fixed verdict: architecture records the conditions of encounter, but those records can be interpreted in more than one way. The Giant Wild Goose Pagoda emerged through pilgrimage, Buddhist transmission, local craft, and Tang patronage. The Palace of Culture and Science emerged through Soviet-led state planning, Polish participation, urban reconstruction, and later public debate. Each case links form to power, but neither can be reduced to power alone.

This matters now because cross-border building remains a central feature of contemporary urban development. China's Belt and Road Initiative, American embassies and university campuses, Gulf-backed urban projects, international museums, and global design consultancies all connect architecture to diplomacy, investment, technical exchange, and institutional influence. The question is not whether architecture should move across borders, but how such movement is negotiated, who participates, who benefits, and how meanings change after construction.

No architectural movement exists entirely outside power relations. At the same time, buildings can be reinterpreted by later users, absorbed into civic life, criticized as symbols of domination, or valued for reasons their original sponsors did not control. The Pagoda and the Palace therefore work best as comparative cases: they invite us to read architecture as a layered field of memory, governance, social relations, and material form.

A century from nowThe buildings being made today may be read through many lenses: as cooperation, ambition, dependency, adaptation, memory, or conflict. The task is to keep those readings open enough to see both the promise and the pressure built into architecture.

A question for our period

How will today's encounters be reinterpreted?

Contemporary projects may support inclusion, identity, cooperation, and shared infrastructure. They may also expose unequal access, institutional leverage, environmental costs, or unresolved histories. Their future meaning will depend not only on design intent, but on governance, maintenance, public use, adaptation, and the communities that inherit them.
